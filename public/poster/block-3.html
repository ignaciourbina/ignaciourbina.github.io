Research design

Pre-registered between-subjects experiment, N = 901 U.S. workers.

A three-step belief-correction protocol. Every respondent sees the expert estimate for their own occupation. 475 treated, 426 control.

Why the intensity varies

Everyone gets the same procedure. The content differs. The size and direction of the gap between belief and expert estimate follow from that respondent's own occupation and prior.

Design property

Both moderators are measured before treatment. The activation corner is therefore a fixed partition of pre-treatment characteristics, not an outcome of the manipulation.

Identification

Expert trust is observed only in the treated arm. A pre-treatment proxy carries it into both. A random forest on 15 pre-treatment terms predicts the trust rating. A logit on that proxy gives the principal score pi. Arm means are then Hájek-weighted by pi for the higher-trust stratum and by 1 − pi for the lower-trust one. The estimand is identified under randomization plus principal ignorability.

Research design

What treated respondents actually saw.

Step 1 · The neutral briefing

Respondents first read a short explanation of how experts assess automation risk. The briefing frames risk around tasks, not occupations. AI systems pick up tasks that are routine, clearly defined and repeated, and that leave many recorded examples to learn from. The shorter and more repetitive the task, the easier it is to automate.

Two jobs from the same field make the contrast concrete: an IT help desk technician handling ten-minute scripted tickets is at higher risk, since chatbots can take most of those tickets; an IT systems designer planning a year of infrastructure is at lower risk, because the work needs long-term planning and team buy-in.

Raises risk

Work that can be done entirely online, or that has abundant recorded data for AI to learn from.

Lowers risk

Work requiring in-person skill or dexterity, or relying on human trust and empathy.

A factual check follows, asking which kind of task is easiest for AI to automate. 93.5% of treated respondents answer correctly, so the briefing is doing its job before any personalized information arrives.

Step 2 · Belief elicitation on a common scale

The instrument asks for a rank, not a probability. Imagine every worker in the country standing in one long line, sorted by how likely their job is to be automated. Position 0 holds the safest jobs. Position 100 holds the jobs at risk soonest. Position 50 has half the workforce ahead and half behind.

Respondents place their own job on that line with a slider from 0 to 100. They then report how many positions their placement might sit above or below the expert's. That records prior uncertainty alongside the point belief.

Step 3 · The named source, its method, and the personalized reveal

The screen names the expert source. The estimates come from Dr. Carl Benedikt Frey and Dr. Michael A. Osborne, researchers at the University of Oxford who study the labor-market impact of AI and automation.

The same screen discloses the method. The researchers began with the U.S. government's O*NET database of skills and daily tasks for every job. A panel of AI researchers tagged 70 sample jobs as automatable or not yet automatable. A model then learned the pattern. It scores jobs heavy on routine, rule-based tasks, fine hand-eye work or data processing higher, and jobs relying on creative thinking, social insight or physical dexterity lower. The result is a 0-to-100 risk score per occupation.

The reveal is personal and explicit. The screen shows the respondent's own placement beside the expert position for their occupation, then reads that score in plain language as a rank among 100 workers.

Why this design creates a genuine shock

Naming the source and explaining its method lets a respondent accept or discount it on the merits. That is what makes expert trust a meaningful gate. Tying the reveal to the respondent's occupation varies the size and direction of the surprise without varying the procedure.

The gap runs wide in both directions, so the treatment is not a one-sided alarm. Roughly as many respondents learn their risk is lower than they thought as higher. Self-reported belief updating tracks the size of the gap. The treatment therefore delivers information, not confirmation.

Outcome and moderators

Policy demand combines five 7-point items on how to respond to AI-driven automation: expanding unemployment benefits and income subsidies; stricter federal regulation of AI deployment; funding retraining programs; strengthening labor protections; and limiting how many jobs AI may automate within companies. The battery spans redistribution, regulation, retraining, labor protections and employment limits, so it does not anchor to a single ideological frame (ω = 0.83, scored by a graded response model).

Expert trust asks after treatment how far the respondent considers the Oxford researchers a trustworthy source on job automation risk. Trait anxiety is a four-item pre-treatment scale (ω = 0.92). Automation risk is the expert percentile for the respondent's own occupation, fixed before the experiment.

The trust proxy

A random forest (500 trees, depth ≤ 10, minimum leaf size 5) is trained on the treated arm to predict the five-point trust rating from 15 pre-treatment terms: education, numeracy, age, race, gender, Democrat and liberal indicators, and the automation-risk percentile, each as a level; the squares of education, numeracy and age; and the interactions age × race, age × education, race × education and numeracy × education.

The prediction, standardized over the full sample, is the proxy Q̂. It is defined in both arms because every input is pre-treatment. A binary logit of high-trust status (trust ≥ 4) on Q̂ yields pi = Pr(high trust | Q̂i). The higher-trust effect is the difference in self-normalized weighted arm means of η with weights pi. The lower-trust effect uses 1 − pi. No post-treatment variable enters the weights, so the same weights apply in the control arm.

Balance

No covariate difference survives Holm correction. Trait anxiety is the largest standardized mean difference at 0.144 (p = 0.031, Holm p = 0.400), followed by age at 0.120 and ideology at −0.110. Those three are the adjustment set for the covariate-adjusted variant of the trust-stratum estimates.

The assumption, stated plainly

Principal ignorability is untestable in this design, because expert trust was never measured in the control arm. A design measuring trust before treatment would estimate the missing quantity directly.
