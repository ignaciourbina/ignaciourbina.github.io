Research design

Pre-registered between-subjects experiment, N = 901 U.S. workers.

A three-step belief-correction protocol whose intensity is individualized: every respondent is shown the expert estimate for their own occupation. 475 treated, 426 control.

The manipulation, step by step

  1. Explain how experts judge automation risk.A neutral briefing: tasks that are routine, clearly defined and repeated, with plenty of recorded examples, are easier to automate. Illustrated with two contrasting jobs, and with what raises risk (fully online work, abundant training data) against what lowers it (in-person dexterity, reliance on trust or empathy). Comprehension is then tested: 93.5% pass.
  2. Elicit the respondent's own belief.Every U.S. worker is described as standing in one line ordered by automation risk, from position 0 (safest) to position 100 (riskiest), with 50 as the midpoint. Respondents place their own job on that line with a slider, then report how far off they think they might be.
  3. Reveal the expert estimate and display the gap.The source is named: Frey and Osborne at the University of Oxford, with their method described. The respondent's own placement and the expert position for their occupation are then shown side by side, with the difference stated in plain terms.
Why the intensity varies

The treatment is the same procedure for everyone, but its content is personalized: the size and direction of the gap between belief and expert estimate is whatever that respondent's own occupation and prior produce.

Design property

Both moderators are measured before treatment, so the activation corner is a fixed partition of pre-treatment characteristics rather than an outcome of the manipulation.

Identification

Expert trust is observed only in the treated arm. A pre-treatment proxy carries it into both: a random forest on 15 pre-treatment terms predicts the trust rating, a logit on that proxy gives the principal score pi, and arm means are Hájek-weighted by pi for the higher-trust stratum and by 1 − pi for the lower-trust one. Identified under randomization plus principal ignorability.

Research design

What treated respondents actually saw.

Step 1 · The neutral briefing

Respondents first read a short explanation of how experts assess automation risk, framed around tasks rather than occupations: when tasks are routine, clearly defined and follow repeated patterns, and there are many recorded examples for a computer to learn from, AI systems can pick them up more easily. The shorter and more repetitive the task, the easier it is to automate.

Two jobs from the same field make the contrast concrete: an IT help desk technician handling ten-minute scripted tickets is at higher risk, since chatbots can take most of those tickets; an IT systems designer planning a year of infrastructure is at lower risk, because the work needs long-term planning and team buy-in.

Raises risk

Work that can be done entirely online, or that has abundant recorded data for AI to learn from.

Lowers risk

Work requiring in-person skill or dexterity, or relying on human trust and empathy.

A factual check follows, asking which kind of task is easiest for AI to automate. 93.5% of treated respondents answer correctly, so the briefing is doing its job before any personalized information arrives.

Step 2 · Belief elicitation on a common scale

Rather than asking for a probability, the instrument uses a ranking metaphor: imagine every worker in the country standing in one long line, sorted by how likely their job is to be automated. Position 0 is for jobs safer than almost everyone else's; position 100 for those at risk sooner than almost everyone else's; position 50 has half the workforce ahead and half behind.

Respondents place their own job on that line with a slider from 0 to 100. They are then asked how many positions above or below their placement might be off from where experts would put their job, which records prior uncertainty alongside the point belief.

Step 3 · The named source, its method, and the personalized reveal

The expert source is not anonymous. Respondents are told the estimates come from Dr. Carl Benedikt Frey and Dr. Michael A. Osborne, researchers at the University of Oxford who study the labor-market impact of AI and automation.

The method is disclosed in the same screen: the researchers began with the U.S. government's O*NET database of skills and daily tasks for every job; a panel of AI researchers first tagged 70 sample jobs as automatable or not yet automatable; a model then learned the pattern, scoring jobs heavy on routine, rule-based tasks, fine hand-eye work or data processing higher, and jobs relying on creative thinking, social insight or physical dexterity lower. The result is a 0-to-100 risk score per occupation.

The reveal is then personal and explicit: the respondent's own placement and the expert position for their occupation are shown together, followed by a plain-language reading of the expert score as a rank among 100 workers.

Why this design creates a genuine shock

Because the source is named and its method explained, a respondent can accept or discount it on its merits, which is what makes expert trust a meaningful gate. Because the reveal is occupation-specific, the size and direction of the surprise vary across respondents without varying the procedure.

The gap is wide in both directions, so the treatment is not a one-sided alarm: roughly as many respondents learn their risk is lower than they thought as higher. Self-reported belief updating tracks the size of the gap, which is what makes it a genuine informational shock rather than a confirmation exercise.

Outcome and moderators

Policy demand is five 7-point items on responses to AI-driven automation's workforce impact: expanding unemployment benefits and income subsidies; stricter federal regulation of AI deployment; funding retraining programs; strengthening labor protections; and limiting how many jobs AI may automate within companies. The battery deliberately spans redistribution, regulation, retraining, labor protections and employment limits, to avoid anchoring to a single ideological frame (ω = 0.83, scored by a graded response model).

Expert trust asks, after treatment, to what extent the respondent thinks the Oxford researchers are a trustworthy source of information about job automation risk. Trait anxiety is a four-item pre-treatment scale (ω = 0.92). Automation risk is the expert percentile for the respondent's own occupation, fixed before the experiment.

The trust proxy

A random forest (500 trees, depth ≤ 10, minimum leaf size 5) is trained on the treated arm to predict the five-point trust rating from 15 pre-treatment terms: education, numeracy, age, race, gender, Democrat and liberal indicators, and the automation-risk percentile, each as a level; the squares of education, numeracy and age; and the interactions age × race, age × education, race × education and numeracy × education.

Its prediction, standardized over the full sample, is the proxy Q̂, defined in both arms because every input is pre-treatment. A binary logit of high-trust status (trust ≥ 4) on Q̂ yields pi = Pr(high trust | Q̂i). The higher-trust effect is the difference in self-normalized weighted arm means of η with weights pi; the lower-trust effect uses 1 − pi. Nothing post-treatment enters the weights, which is why the same weights can be applied in the control arm.

Balance

No covariate difference survives Holm correction. Trait anxiety is the largest standardized mean difference at 0.144 (p = 0.031, Holm p = 0.400), followed by age at 0.120 and ideology at −0.110. Those three are the adjustment set for the covariate-adjusted variant of the trust-stratum estimates.

The assumption, stated plainly

Principal ignorability is untestable in this design, because expert trust was never measured in the control arm. A design measuring trust before treatment would estimate the missing quantity directly.
