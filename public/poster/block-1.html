The puzzle

Automation exposure is widespread, yet experimental evidence on policy demand remains inconclusive.

Frey & Osborne (2017) estimate 47% of U.S. occupations face high automation risk. Public awareness is high (Pew 2023), but experiments linking risk information to policy preferences yield mixed results.

  • Structural labor market shift.Routine cognitive and manual tasks face increasing automation pressure.
  • Policy demand channel.Standard models predict risk exposure → demand for retraining, regulation, redistribution.
  • Empirical gap.Experimental tests of this channel produce inconsistent or null aggregate effects.

Where the evidence stands

Zhang & Hou (2022); Magistro et al. (2024). Personalized risk information → null ATE on policy preferences. Gallego et al. (2022). Distinct treatment and sample yield positive effects. Arntz et al. (2024). Opposing subgroup effects cancel in the aggregate. Wu (2022); Im (2023). Effects moderated by ideology, education, prior beliefs.
Reframing the estimand

The unconditional ATE averages over heterogeneous subgroups. Weak aggregate effects may reflect composition rather than preference stability.
