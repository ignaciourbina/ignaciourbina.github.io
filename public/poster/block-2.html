Theory & hypotheses

Three traditions converge: personal risk information should increase policy demand.

Self-interest theory, threat-motivated cognition, and egotropic economic voting each predict that learning one's occupation faces automation risk should increase support for protective policies.

Self-interest. Workers facing losses seek compensatory policy (Meltzer & Richard 1981). Threat-motivated cognition. Threat perception focuses attention on protection (Albertson & Gadarian 2015). Egotropic voting. Individual economic exposure drives policy demand (Kinder & Kiewiet 1981).

The Conditional Activation Model

Two sequential gates: expert trust, then joint activation (anxiety × automation risk). Trust governs whether the expert signal is absorbed at all. Activation requires dispositional vigilance and personal exposure together, not either alone.

  • Gate 1: expert trust.Whether the signal is absorbed at all.
  • Gate 2: joint requirement.Neither trait anxiety nor own-occupation automation risk alone should be sufficient to move policy demand.
  • Below-threshold pathway.Beliefs update without policy preference change (cool cognition).

Predictions

H1 · Trust gate

The treatment effect on policy demand is larger among respondents who trust the expert source than among those who do not.

H2 · Conjunctive activation

Among those who absorb the signal, the effect concentrates where high trait anxiety meets high own-occupation automation risk, rather than where either is high alone.

H3 · Composition

If the three pathways are mixed in the population, the unconditional ATE will understate the responsive subgroup, producing the weak or null aggregate effects observed in prior work.
