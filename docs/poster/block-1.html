The diagnosis

Weak aggregate effects may reflect composition rather than preference stability.

The puzzle

Experimental results are inconsistent: null, positive, and opposing effects coexist.

Frey & Osborne (2017) estimate 47% of U.S. occupations face high automation risk. Public awareness is high (Pew 2023), but experiments linking risk information to policy preferences yield mixed results.

  • Structural labor market shift.Routine cognitive and manual tasks face increasing automation pressure.
  • Policy demand channel.Standard models predict risk exposure → demand for retraining, regulation, redistribution.
  • Empirical gap.Experimental tests of this channel produce inconsistent or null aggregate effects.

Prior evidence

Zhang & Hou (2022); Magistro et al. (2024). Personalized risk information → null ATE on policy preferences. Gallego et al. (2022). Distinct treatment and sample yield positive effects. Arntz et al. (2024). Opposing subgroup effects cancel in the aggregate. Wu (2022); Im (2023). Effects moderated by ideology, education, prior beliefs.

Studies examining conditional effects find heterogeneity that washes out in the aggregate.

Reframing the estimand

The unconditional ATE averages over heterogeneous subgroups, so the estimand is misspecified for the question at hand. Weak aggregate effects may reflect composition rather than preference stability. The relevant estimand conditions on who absorbs the signal and on who is personally exposed.

Standard estimand

E[Y(1) − Y(0)]: unconditional ATE across the full sample

Proposed estimand

E[Y(1) − Y(0) | trust stratum, anxiety × automation risk]: CATE by expert-trust stratum and by joint exposure

A weak population-level ATE is the expected signature when qualitatively distinct pathways are mixed. In this study the aggregate effect is not itself null: it is positive but modest, and it understates the group that moves.
