The diagnosis

Weak averages may reflect who responds. They do not show that preferences are fixed.

The puzzle

Experiments disagree. They report null, positive and opposing effects.

Frey & Osborne (2017) estimate that 47% of U.S. occupations face high automation risk. Pew (2023) reports high public awareness. Experiments linking risk information to policy preferences still disagree.

  • Structural labor market shift.Routine cognitive and manual tasks face rising automation pressure.
  • Policy demand channel.Standard models predict that risk exposure raises demand for retraining, regulation and redistribution.
  • Empirical gap.Experimental tests of that channel return inconsistent or null averages.

Prior evidence

Zhang & Hou (2022); Magistro et al. (2024). Personalized risk information produced no average effect on policy preferences. Gallego et al. (2022). A different treatment and sample produced positive effects. Arntz et al. (2024). Opposing subgroup effects cancelled in the average. Wu (2022); Im (2023). Effects varied by ideology, education and prior beliefs.

Reframing the estimand

The unconditional ATE averages over subgroups that respond differently, so it answers the wrong question. The right estimand conditions on who absorbs the signal and on who is personally exposed.

Standard estimand

E[Y(1) − Y(0)]: unconditional ATE across the full sample

Proposed estimand

E[Y(1) − Y(0) | trust stratum, anxiety × automation risk]: CATE by expert-trust stratum and by joint exposure

Mixing distinct pathways produces a weak population average. Here the average is not null. It is positive but modest, and it understates the group that moves.
